No. 06 · Design

A roster is not a queue

The turn order was already on screen, already sorted, and already correct. Players still could not read it.

Across the top of a ZA! table sat a row of opponent panels — name, avatar, card count. That row was sorted into true turn order, starting from the player after you. It even reversed itself when somebody played a Flip the Pie. The answer was on the screen, and it was right.

Players kept asking whose turn it was. And who was next.

We were one meeting away from labelling it harder: ordinal badges, a stronger highlight on the active panel, little arrows between panels. More paint on a surface that was not the problem.

A row of equal panels reads as a roster

Four identical boxes in a line say here are the four people in this game. Nothing in that shape says and this is the order they go in. So players did what the shape suggested: they read the active name off the marquee, then hunted along the row for the panel with the matching name. Every turn.

The order was in the data. It was in the DOM. It was not in the shape — and the shape is what people read.

A second loss rode along in the same row: card counts were numerals. “Nonna Pina is down to two” was something you had to read, not something you saw. A number is decoded. A small pile is noticed.

Furniture instead of labels

The chefs now sit around a counter — three dashed sides, open at the near edge, where your own hand already is. Turn order stopped being a property of a sorted list and became where people are sitting. Nobody reads a seating plan. You just see it.

Card counts became physical in the same pass. Up to seven cards is a literal fan of cards. Eight or more becomes a deck with the numeral on its tray, because past a point a fan stops being countable and a stack is the honest picture.

And a chevron token walks from the chef who just played to the chef who is next. A reverse is not a re-sort and not a new label: it is the token turning around and walking back the way it came.

The tell: the fix deleted code

The reversal handling in orderedOpponents() went. So did the entire hard-swap path. Once the seating carries the order, there is nothing left that needs re-ordering — the direction of travel is a property of the token, not of the list.

Order as a list operation

const row = orderedOpponents();
if (reversed) row.reverse();
// + a hard-swap path

Order as furniture

// seats never move.
// the token walks the other way.

We proved that nothing moved the way you have to prove these things: by asserting every seat’s offsetLeft and offsetTop, and the pile’s rect, byte-identical across a turn advance and a direction flip. Not by looking at it.

That deletion is the strongest signal in this whole zine. When a structural fix is right, it usually takes code away, because you have stopped simulating with logic something the layout can just be.

The general shape: if players cannot read something that is already on screen, adding labels treats the symptom. The visual language is throwing the information away before it reaches them. Change the shape.

HOUSE RULE

If people cannot read what is already on screen, change the shape, not the labels — and the right structural fix usually deletes code.
